Maritime Security & Shipping

Severe ↑ Confidence: High

  • IMF PortWatch 3.9 transits/day w/e 8 Nov; AIS-visible 6–7/day mid-November (hormuz.now); JMIC "single-digit to low-double-digit" per day; ~211 vessels at anchor east of Hormuz (JMIC 12 Nov). US-escorted UANI count ~32/day Nov 8–11 — highest-quality commercial flow estimate.

Why it matters: The Strait of Hormuz remains effectively closed to unescorted commercial traffic. The 20-day consecutive UKMTO incident cadence is structurally sustained, not episodic. Three-carrier CENTCOM configuration as of 15 Nov creates both a deterrent posture and an escalation surface — IRGC mine strikes on US-escorted vessels now carry direct conflict-widening risk.

Supply chain implication: Any cargo or commodity on a >30-day forward contract priced on Gulf transit assumptions requires reassessment. War-risk insurance at 7–9% hull value makes unescorted transits economically irrational. US-escorted convoys at 32/day represent the effective throughput ceiling. Cape of Good Hope diversion adds 10–14 days transit; freight rates elevated. Refined product and LNG buyers in Asia should assume rolling disruption through at least EOD 22 Nov (Roosevelt kinetic trigger window).

Sources: UKMTO Warning 201-26; UANI shipping update 13 Nov; JMIC 12 Nov; hormuz.now 16 Nov; Lloyd's List 9 Nov; Windward 13 Nov; gCaptain 10 Nov.

Energy Infrastructure

Stressed → Confidence: Medium
  • Kpler estimated ~8.3 mb/d oil/product flows through Hormuz week to 11 Nov (preliminary, includes dark-ship transits under US escort corridor) — structurally reduced but not zero; Iranian oil continues flowing to sanctioned-route buyers.
  • Petroline (East-West Pipeline) operating at reduced capacity; Yanbu Red Sea terminal operational but upstream throughput constrained. No Hard-confirmed new Yanbu physical damage as of 06:00 UTC 16 Nov.
  • War-risk insurance 7–9% hull value (Lloyd's List 9 Nov) — three-carrier CENTCOM configuration will likely widen premium further. Iran blacklisted 240 ships (Windward 13 Nov). ADNOC vessel damaged UAE territorial waters 10 Nov. JMIC maintains Hormuz threat level SEVERE.

Why it matters: The effective closure of Hormuz to unescorted commercial traffic represents the largest sustained energy infrastructure disruption since the 1973 oil embargo in terms of chokepoint duration. Dark-ship and US-escorted flows at ~8.3 mb/d (Kpler) versus pre-crisis ~21 mb/d imply a ~60% volumetric reduction in transparent/accountable flow. The resumed IRGC Bahrain strikes and IRGC mine campaign simultaneously threaten the escort infrastructure itself.

Supply chain implication: Refined product buyers without long-term contracted volumes should assume constrained supply through at least Q1 2027. The Yanbu no-damage status is a critical floor — any Hard-confirmed Yanbu strike + ≥24h zero-load (Kpler) triggers Threat-5 immediately. Petroline reduced capacity means Saudi East-West diversion is already saturated. LNG buyers in Asia should model a 30-day rolling worst-case with no new Hormuz transit capacity.

Sources: Kpler 13 Nov (hormuz.now); Lloyd's List/Insurance Business 9 Nov; Windward 13 Nov; JMIC 12 Nov; UANI 13 Nov; gCaptain 10 Nov.

Alternative Routes & Chokepoints

Degraded ↑ Confidence: High
  • Southern Armed Forces (Yemen internationally recognized government) withdrew from Dhubab Airport and Bab al-Mandeb area 15 Nov 2026 (Reuters/Al-Monitor) — announced as "redeployment to secure Aden"; ends six-week counteroffensive that had partially contested Houthi Red Sea coast control since September 2026.
  • Houthis now control territory overlooking Bab al-Mandeb strait; prospect of advance toward Aden raised; Taiz besieged with ~300,000 displaced; Houthis positioned ~80km from Tur al-Bahah (Reuters 15 Nov). Saudi Arabia struck Houthi targets in Yemen response. Houthis released 600 Saudi-backed fighters under amnesty 15 Nov — potential leverage play.
  • Cape of Good Hope diversion route operating at capacity — primary bypass for both Hormuz and Red Sea closure. Suez Canal northbound throughput constrained by southern Red Sea Houthi environment. Northern rail (China-Europe) at saturation. No viable commercial alternative to Hormuz or Bab al-Mandeb within the 30-day outlook horizon.

Why it matters: The simultaneous effective closure of Hormuz (Iranian mine campaign, 20 consecutive UKMTO days) and degradation of Bab al-Mandeb (Houthi consolidation post-15 Nov withdrawal) is the dual-chokepoint scenario that supply chain planners had assessed as a tail risk. It is now the base case. Cape of Good Hope is the only remaining route — and it cannot absorb full combined Hormuz + Red Sea diverted volume at current freight rates and vessel availability.

Supply chain implication: Dual-chokepoint base case means Cape of Good Hope route is operating at or near saturation for heavy cargo. Transit time penalty 10–14 days for Hormuz bypass, 7–10 days for Red Sea bypass — cumulative 17–24 days vs direct routing. Any further Houthi advance toward Aden or resumed maritime interdiction in Red Sea will trigger freight rate spikes and cargo booking failures on affected lanes. Importers on affected lanes should model 30-45 day buffer depletion and contingency sourcing from non-Gulf origins.

Sources: Reuters/Al-Monitor 15 Nov; The National 15 Nov; CNBC 15 Nov; brecorder 16 Nov; BusinessWorld 16 Nov; hormuz.now 16 Nov.

Methodology

This brief uses a tiered signal-weighting framework. Only verified physical events from primary sources can move the threat assessment; trend movement requires confirmed change versus the prior 72h. The system continuously calibrates against outcomes — when predictions miss, the underlying heuristics are sharpened. The full operating ruleset is kept private to preserve analytical edge against adversarial reading.

Sources

Inputs span six tiers, weighted by track record. Specific outlets and platforms are not disclosed publicly.

  • Tier 1 — Primary & official. Maritime authorities, defense commands, foreign ministries, treasury sanctions desks. First-party communiqués that count as Hard signal.
  • Tier 2 — Specialized commercial intelligence. Vessel-tracking platforms (AIS-based), aircraft-tracking platforms (ADS-B), tanker-flow analytics, energy-cargo intelligence, piracy-reporting bureaus. Operational data with measurable track records.
  • Tier 3 — Analytical institutions. Geopolitical and security think tanks with rigorous publication standards and demonstrable calibration history on this region.
  • Tier 4 — Commercial analysts & columnists. Independent practitioners and commodity-desk analysts whose published positions can be back-tested over multi-year horizons.
  • Tier 5 — Wire services & reputable regional press. Western wire services, top-tier business and political dailies, regional papers across the Gulf, Israel, and Iran (read with editorial-line caveats), and analytical defense-and-policy publications.
  • Tier 6 — Excluded by default. Anonymous OSINT accounts, op-eds, social media, "sources say" without attribution, AI-generated summaries, forum content. Enter the brief only after independent corroboration by Tier 1–3.
