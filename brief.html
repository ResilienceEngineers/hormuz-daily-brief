The Khasab zone (6–13nm N) has been the consistent targeting area across five incidents. No confirmed sinking; crew safety record suggests deterrent/harassment rather than destruction-priority posture — but escalation to mine deployment remains unverified and structurally possible.

Supply-chain implication: AIS-visible commercial transits at ~2% of pre-crisis baseline (PortWatch); US-facilitated corridor at 27.7/day averaging ~32% of pre-crisis 85/day commercial baseline. Tanker owners pricing in UKMTO SEVERE rating; war-risk premiums structurally elevated. Cargo diversions to Cape route adding ~14 days transit time; refinery planning horizons compressed.

At Risk → Confidence: Medium

  • Brent crude $91.68 (18 Nov close, Bloomberg); down ~16% over 30 days driven by structural supply-surplus pressure despite war risk. 52-week high $118.35 (Mar 2026). EIA Q4 2026 Brent forecast $105/bbl. Oil is structurally ~44% above year-ago price — war premium embedded but supply surplus capping upside.
  • Kpler (via hormuz.now 13 Nov): Hormuz oil flows ~8.3 mb/d (7-day avg to 11 Nov) vs ~17.1 mb/d pre-war — ~48% of pre-war level. Petroline (East-West Pipeline) running at reduced capacity as Hormuz bypass. Two Iranian STS crude transfers confirmed in Strait 18 Nov (UANI satellite imagery).

Structural driver: The gap between war-risk severity and oil-price suppression reflects a supply-surplus structural overhang absorbing disruption. If IRGC shifts retaliation vector to Saudi/UAE energy infrastructure (a plausible next rung on the escalation ladder), this dynamic reverses sharply — Ras Tanura alone handles ~6–7% of global seaborne crude.

Supply-chain implication: Maintain precautionary inventory buffers for Middle East-origin crude and refined products. The structural supply surplus provides a 2–4 week cushion against a single-event disruption, but a Ras Tanura shutdown would exhaust this buffer rapidly. Watch for IRGC shift from base-striking to energy-infrastructure targeting as the escalation ladder next rung.

Alternative Routes

Stressed → Confidence: High
  • Bab al-Mandeb: Houthis hold full ~150 km Red Sea coastline from Dhubab to Khor Amira following PLC withdrawal 15 Nov 2026 (AFP/Al Jazeera confirmed). Two Houthi military sources confirmed deployment to evacuated Red Sea areas. Suez Canal effectively closed to Hormuz-origin and Red Sea traffic. Suez Canal Authority revenue impact ongoing.
  • Aden ground situation: Houthis positioned ~100 km west of city per The National (18 Nov, citing Yemeni security official); contrast with AFP/ToI (15 Nov): 60 km from city. Discrepancy likely reflects contested front lines and dating. ISW (18 Nov): government forces "unable to open new fronts, stretch Houthi resources, or regain the initiative." Security sources and experts told The National an imminent land assault is "unlikely" — siege scenario more probable.
  • Cape of Good Hope: primary commercial diversion active; +~14 days transit vs Suez; structural cost elevation (bunker fuel, insurance, time-charter rates). No capacity ceiling breach reported yet but volume is elevated from both Hormuz and Red Sea diversions.

Structural driver: Both primary chokepoints (Hormuz and BaM) are simultaneously degraded — Hormuz to ~2% commercial AIS baseline, BaM to near-zero commercial transits. This is the dual-chokepoint disruption scenario. The Cape route is functioning as a release valve but adding structural cost. The Petroline bypass provides partial GCC crude export capacity but cannot replace Hormuz volume at current reduced throughput.

Supply-chain implication: Cape routing is now the operational default for all Middle East-origin and Red Sea-transit cargo. Freight rates and charter rates structurally elevated. Extend forward Cape-route booking horizon; hedge bunker-fuel exposure. If Aden falls or is besieged, Saudi Red Sea port access (Jeddah) becomes more critical as a secondary routing hub.

Sources: AFP (via France 24 / Times of Israel) 15–17 Nov 2026 · Al Jazeera 16 Nov 2026 · The National 18 Nov 2026 · ISW analysis (cited by The National) 18 Nov 2026

Methodology

This brief uses a tiered signal-weighting framework. Only verified physical events from primary sources can move the threat assessment; trend movement requires confirmed change versus the prior 72h. The system continuously calibrates against outcomes — when predictions miss, the underlying heuristics are sharpened. The full operating ruleset is kept private to preserve analytical edge against adversarial reading.

Sources

Inputs span six tiers, weighted by track record. Specific outlets and platforms are not disclosed publicly.

  • Tier 1 — Primary & official. Maritime authorities, defense commands, foreign ministries, treasury sanctions desks. First-party communiqués that count as Hard signal.
  • Tier 2 — Specialized commercial intelligence. Vessel-tracking platforms (AIS-based), aircraft-tracking platforms (ADS-B), tanker-flow analytics, energy-cargo intelligence, piracy-reporting bureaus. Operational data with measurable track records.
  • Tier 3 — Analytical institutions. Geopolitical and security think tanks with rigorous publication standards and demonstrable calibration history on this region.
  • Tier 4 — Commercial analysts & columnists. Independent practitioners and commodity-desk analysts whose published positions can be back-tested over multi-year horizons.
  • Tier 5 — Wire services & reputable regional press. Western wire services, top-tier business and political dailies, regional papers across the Gulf, Israel, and Iran (read with editorial-line caveats), and analytical defense-and-policy publications.
  • Tier 6 — Excluded by default. Anonymous OSINT accounts, op-eds, social media, "sources say" without attribution, AI-generated summaries, forum content. Enter the brief only after independent corroboration by Tier 1–3.
