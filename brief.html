Maritime Security — Strait of Hormuz

Severe ↑ Confidence: High (≥3 Tier 1–2 sources)
  • UKMTO three-incident cluster (16–17 Nov): Tanker struck by unknown projectile outbound Hormuz (engine fire, all crew safe); second tanker disabled 7nm N of Khasab Oman (engine fire, crew safe); third tanker attacked in Hormuz (engine fire, crew safe). Three independent UKMTO alerts within ~18 hours — highest single-day density since war onset.
  • IRGC supertanker sinking claim (15 Nov, unconfirmed): IRGC claims VLCC carrying >2 million barrels crude sank in southern Persian Gulf after mine strike on unauthorised route. Iranian state media cites 5 tankers struck in preceding 48h. No CENTCOM/UKMTO vessel-identity confirmation of sinking as of 06:00 UTC 17 Nov.
  • IMF PortWatch transit baseline: ~3 transits/day recorded 8 Nov vs pre-crisis baseline of 85/day. AIS shows 36 vessels holding position outside Hormuz/Gulf watch-box as of 02:30 UTC 17 Nov. Structural closure of corridor to commercial traffic confirmed.

Why it matters: Three Hard-confirmed UKMTO incidents in 18 hours indicates mine campaign phase-shift — from episodic harassment to sustained denial-of-passage operations targeting any vessel on CENTCOM-escorted routes. The unconfirmed supertanker sinking, if validated, would constitute the largest single-incident cargo loss since war onset and generate immediate environmental and insurance force-majeure events across the tanker market.

Supply-chain implication: Tanker owners increasingly refusing Hormuz transit regardless of CENTCOM escort. Effective cargo availability for Asia-bound crude via Hormuz now near zero. Insurance war-risk premiums at multi-year highs. Vessel allocation for Cape rerouting under severe pressure — mine campaign attrition directly reduces available fleet. Procurement teams should assume Hormuz-dependent cargoes undeliverable on existing schedules through end-November minimum.

Sources: UKMTO alerts (Tier 1) × 3; Middle East Eye live blog (Tier 2); Yahoo/Maritime Executive (Tier 2); straits.live tracker (Tier 3); IRGC/IRNA (Tier 5, corroborated by UKMTO for incident existence)

Military Posture — US/Israel/IRGC

Severe ↑ Confidence: High (Axios/ABC/CENTCOM; KAN; Bloomberg)
  • Three-carrier CENTCOM configuration live (15 Nov): USS Theodore Roosevelt (CVN-71) entered CENTCOM AOR Sunday 15 Nov confirmed by US official via Axios/Barak Ravid; joins USS George H.W. Bush (CSG-10, in theater since April) and USS George Washington (since August). ~144 front-line strike fighters plus Tomahawk-equipped escorts now in theater. Rotation vs supplementation unresolved per Stars and Stripes/The National.
  • IRGC Bahrain strikes (13 + 15 Nov): IRGC launched 2 drones at Al-Riffa helicopter base 13 Nov (CENTCOM confirmed both shot down); launched 6 drones 15 Nov (Bahrain air defense intercepted all, IRGC claimed "successful impact"). First Bahrain strikes since early September. IRGC framing: retaliation for US "aerial provocations and oil smuggling in Hormuz."
  • Israel security cabinet readiness directive (11 Nov): KAN reported security cabinet directed IDF to increase readiness for Iran strike; IDF Chief Zamir repeatedly opposed unilateral action; Eisenkot constraint on caretaker government actions; no formal strike authorization as of 17 Nov. Gofman returned to Israel 15 Nov having briefed Ratcliffe + Witkoff.

Why it matters: Three-carrier CENTCOM posture is the clearest observable precursor to Threat-5 kinetic window. Combined with IRGC resumption of Bahrain strikes and Israel security cabinet readiness directive, the structural prerequisites for escalation are assembled. The sole brake — Trump "hold off" + Gulf state requests for delay — is under sustained IRGC pressure testing. No Trump retaliatory statement issued as of 06:00 UTC 17 Nov, but each IRGC Bahrain attack narrows the political space for continued restraint.

Supply-chain implication: Three-carrier config is a 7–14 day decision-forcing event: either Trump authorizes resumed strikes (Scenario C) or begins carrier rotation signaling stand-down (Scenario B partial anchor). Until resolved, procurement planning horizon should cap at 14 days for Hormuz-dependent and Gulf-sourced commodities. Israeli unilateral strike remains lower probability but would trigger Iranian retaliation on Gulf-state energy infrastructure within the brief's threat model.

Sources: Axios/Barak Ravid (Tier 2); CENTCOM statement (Tier 1); Bloomberg (Tier 2); KAN/Times of Israel (Tier 2); Al Jazeera (Tier 2)

Diplomatic Track — Iran/US/Oman/Qatar

Elevated → Confidence: Medium (Oman FM tweet; APA; IRNA; multiple Tier 2)
  • Araghchi–Busaidi Muscat meeting (17 Nov confirmed): Araghchi met Omani FM Badr Albusaidi in Muscat 17 Nov per APA, Middle East Eye, Muscat Daily. Oman FM readout (X post): "constructive meeting … reviewed steady course of Oman–Iran relations … exchanged views on latest diplomatic efforts … reaffirmed importance of de-escalation and peaceful solutions." No public joint communiqué, no Hormuz-specific terms announced.
  • Gharibabadi two-day Hormuz plan reiterated (16 Nov): Iran Deputy FM Gharibabadi stated two-day Hormuz reopening plan remains on table, contingent on US meeting all 7 conditions. Original 7-day plan reduced to 2 days following mediator consultations. Gharibabadi: US must rebuild trust; Iran prepared for "three scenarios" (diplomacy, blockade, military). Araghchi told press future of diplomacy depends on US "political rationality."
  • Counter-signal — IRGC dual-track (structural): IRGC resumed Bahrain drone strikes simultaneously with Araghchi Pakistan/Oman trip. Iran's "door to peace still open" statement (Bloomberg 16 Nov) is consistent with IRGC using military pressure to strengthen diplomatic leverage — not evidence of imminent agreement. Qatar-mediated channel active but no draft text confirmed publicly.

Why it matters: The Araghchi Oman visit is the most substantive diplomatic observable in the current cycle. Oman's framing ("peaceful solutions," "de-escalation") is consistent with Muscat actively relaying US-Iran indirect messages — Oman has historically been the primary back-channel for US-Iran nuclear and security talks. Absence of a joint communiqué is consistent with quiet mediation, not failure. The Gharibabadi two-day plan remains the operational offer on the table; US acknowledgment via Rubio/CENTCOM would be the first observable indicator of Scenario A.

Supply-chain implication: Araghchi Oman meeting without outcome keeps Scenario A probability at 10% for now. If Oman produces a US-relayed acknowledgment within 48 hours, scenario probability revision upward is warranted. Procurement teams should not plan around diplomatic resolution in the current 7-day window; monitor Rubio/State Department statements on Hormuz conditions as the leading indicator.

Sources: APA Tehran bureau (Tier 2); Middle East Eye live blog (Tier 2); Muscat Daily (Tier 3); Jpost/IRNA (Tier 2/5); Bloomberg/Rigzone (Tier 2); The Statesman/Tribune (Tier 3)

Energy Infrastructure — Gulf & Red Sea

Elevated → Confidence: Medium (Kpler/PortWatch; multiple Tier 2–3)
  • Saudi East–West pipeline / Yanbu (operating): No confirmed strike on East–West pipeline or Yanbu terminal in current cycle. Houthi consolidation of Bab al-Mandeb raises potential Yanbu strike-range risk if Red Sea approaches deteriorate further. Yanbu is the downstream endpoint of the pipeline bypass around Hormuz — its protection is structurally critical to Saudi export continuity.
  • Qatar LNG / ADNOC Fujairah (operating, elevated risk): Qatar LNG rerouted via Cape; ADNOC Fujairah pipeline bypass of Hormuz operational. No direct strikes on either facility confirmed in current cycle. Insurance premiums elevated for Gulf-coast loading operations.
  • Brent crude ~$95/bbl (16 Nov): Down 0.37% session per straits.live/PortWatch data. Elevated but contained — strategic reserve drawdowns and demand destruction in EM markets providing partial offset. Below the $105–110 threshold associated with prior Threat-5 price signals.

Why it matters: No confirmed infrastructure strike in current cycle keeps energy infrastructure at Elevated rather than Severe. The structural risk is cascade: Houthi Aden advance → potential Yanbu strike range → East–West pipeline as last remaining major Saudi export corridor threatened. This cascade is not currently triggered but the prerequisites are assembling. Brent ~$95 reflects market pricing of Scenario B (frozen attrition) — any Hard-confirmed infrastructure strike would gap this significantly higher.

Supply-chain implication: Maintain contingency sourcing for Gulf-originated crude and LNG on 30-day rolling basis. East–West/Yanbu pipeline capacity is the single most important infrastructure observable — monitor Kpler Yanbu loading data daily. Any zero-load signal ≥24h at Yanbu is a Threat-5 candidate event.

Sources: IMF PortWatch (Tier 1); straits.live (Tier 3); Bloomberg energy desk (Tier 2); Kpler (Tier 2)

Alternative Routes — Bab al-Mandeb / Cape / Pipeline

Severe ↑ Confidence: High (Long War Journal; Al Jazeera; Xinhua/Siasat)
  • Houthi Bab al-Mandeb consolidation (15–16 Nov): Southern Armed Forces withdrew from Dhubab Airport and Bab al-Mandeb area 15 Nov; Houthis seized territory along strait and Taiz-Lahj border per Long War Journal (Tier 2). Houthi positions now ~70 km west of Aden per Xinhua/multiple corroboration. Counteroffensive formally declared failed by government military source. Red Sea/Suez route effectively closed to commercial shipping.
  • Cape of Good Hope — primary diversion route, stressed: Cape routing adds 14–21 days per voyage; tanker fleet availability under pressure as mine campaign removes hulls. No Cape-route specific incidents in current cycle, but capacity stress is structural and tightening with each mine-campaign loss.
  • Iraq–Ceyhan pipeline (operating, proxy-risk): Pipeline operating but Iranian proxy pressure on Kurdish transit region elevated. No confirmed shutdown in current cycle. Ceyhan provides alternative crude export corridor for Iraq — monitor for PKK/militia interference as IRGC proxy-coordination signal.

Why it matters: Both primary chokepoints — Hormuz and Bab al-Mandeb — are simultaneously compromised as of 15–16 November. This configuration, last observed briefly at the March 2026 war onset, represents the highest structural supply-chain risk state in this conflict. The Cape route is now the sole viable primary corridor for most energy and container traffic between Asia/Middle East and Europe/Americas. Any Cape-route disruption (Houthi long-range strike capability extending to southern Red Sea approaches, or Iranian proxy coordination) would constitute a full global supply-chain crisis.

Supply-chain implication: Both-chokepoints-disrupted configuration requires immediate review of routing assumptions and lead-time buffers. +14–21 day voyage extension via Cape implies pre-positioned inventory requirements should be revised upward. Suez/Red Sea routing should be treated as unavailable for planning purposes through end-Q1 2027 minimum. Monitor Houthi advance toward Aden as the leading indicator of whether Bab al-Mandeb control becomes permanent vs temporary.

Sources: Long War Journal (Tier 2); Al Jazeera (Tier 2); Xinhua/Siasat (Tier 2–3); theshillongtimes/avenuemail (Tier 3, corroborated by LWJ)

Outlook — Scenario Probabilities & Leading Indicators

Concerning ↑ Confidence: Medium
  • Scenario C re-escalation at 53% (↑+1pp): Three-carrier CENTCOM live; Houthi Bab al-Mandeb consolidation; IRGC Bahrain strike pattern resumed; Israel security cabinet readiness directive active; Araghchi Oman meeting yielded no agreement. Eisenkot constraint on Israeli caretaker government is primary Israeli brake; Trump "hold off" is primary US brake — both under daily pressure testing.
  • Scenario B frozen attrition at 37% (↓−1pp): Trump "hold off" + Gulf state requests for delay remain operative brakes. Qatar/Oman diplomatic channels active. Iran dual-track (escalate + negotiate) is structurally consistent with extending Scenario B while improving Iran's negotiating position. Araghchi Oman meeting without outcome is consistent with Scenario B continuation.
  • Counter-signal to Worse trend: Bloomberg 16 Nov: Iran reiterated "door to peace still open"; Araghchi Oman meeting occurred as scheduled despite IRGC Bahrain strikes — demonstrates Iran's intent to maintain diplomatic optics. Iran's Gharibabadi two-day plan is a standing de-escalation offer that requires only US acknowledgment to trigger. This is the primary argument against Scenario C probability exceeding 60%.

Why it matters: The 53% re-escalation probability reflects a structural assessment, not a 24-hour trigger prediction. The three-carrier CENTCOM posture creates a decision-forcing environment over the next 7–14 days: either Trump authorizes action (confirmed by public statement or observable CENTCOM posture shift), or begins carrier rotation (observable via CENTCOM statement or AIS departure of one CSG). Neither has occurred as of 06:00 UTC 17 Nov.

Supply-chain implication: 53% re-escalation over 30 days implies procurement planning should maintain dual-track posture: Scenario B baseline (Cape routing, elevated insurance, 14–21 day lead-time extension) with Scenario C contingency (full chokepoint closure, strategic reserve activation, emergency sourcing protocols pre-identified). The simultaneous Hormuz + Bab al-Mandeb disruption already constitutes a Scenario C supply-chain condition regardless of military outcome — the question is whether it deepens or partially reverses.

Sources: Bloomberg (Tier 2); Al Jazeera (Tier 2); KAN/Allisrael News (Tier 2); Axios (Tier 2); IMF PortWatch (Tier 1)

Methodology

This brief uses a tiered signal-weighting framework. Only verified physical events from primary sources can move the threat assessment; trend movement requires confirmed change versus the prior 72h. The system continuously calibrates against outcomes — when predictions miss, the underlying heuristics are sharpened. The full operating ruleset is kept private to preserve analytical edge against adversarial reading.

Sources

Inputs span six tiers, weighted by track record. Specific outlets and platforms are not disclosed publicly.

  • Tier 1 — Primary & official. Maritime authorities, defense commands, foreign ministries, treasury sanctions desks. First-party communiqués that count as Hard signal.
  • Tier 2 — Specialized commercial intelligence. Vessel-tracking platforms (AIS-based), aircraft-tracking platforms (ADS-B), tanker-flow analytics, energy-cargo intelligence, piracy-reporting bureaus. Operational data with measurable track records.
  • Tier 3 — Analytical institutions. Geopolitical and security think tanks with rigorous publication standards and demonstrable calibration history on this region.
  • Tier 4 — Commercial analysts & columnists. Independent practitioners and commodity-desk analysts whose published positions can be back-tested over multi-year horizons.
  • Tier 5 — Wire services & reputable regional press. Western wire services, top-tier business and political dailies, regional papers across the Gulf, Israel, and Iran (read with editorial-line caveats), and analytical defense-and-policy publications.
  • Tier 6 — Excluded by default. Anonymous OSINT accounts, op-eds, social media, "sources say" without attribution, AI-generated summaries, forum content. Enter the brief only after independent corroboration by Tier 1–3.
