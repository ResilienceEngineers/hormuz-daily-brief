CENTCOM on 1 September completed a wave of precision strikes against IRGC targets — air-defence sites, radar systems, maritime assets, mine-laying capabilities and communications infrastructure — characterised by a US official as "mowing the lawn," signalling a periodic, indefinite strike tempo rather than a decisive campaign endpoint. Hormuz commercial transits remain at approximately 5–6 vessels per day — roughly 6–7% of the pre-crisis baseline of ~85/day — with the Omani corridor offering no safety premium after twin kinetic attacks on 29–31 Aug.

  • MARAD 2026-004 (Hard T1, expires 9 Sep): Iran continues threats and strikes on commercial vessels in Persian Gulf, Strait of Hormuz and Gulf of Oman; threats now include direct missile attacks, UAVs and USVs; GNSS spoofing persistent (MARAD 2026-008 / JMIC).

Why it matters: The twin Khasab incidents (29–31 Aug) confirmed that CORRIDOR SAFETY PREMIUM IS ELIMINATED — inbound and outbound transits in both the main lane and the Omani corridor face equivalent kinetic risk. AIS suppression (29 of 40 anchored vessels dark, east Hormuz anchorage per Windward T2 31 Aug) masks true exposure. Iranian 56-vessel shipping blacklist (Baird Maritime via Straits.live T3) creates additional targeting uncertainty for non-blacklisted vessels attempting transit.

Military

Stated rationale: IRGC attempted attacks on commercial shipping and US service members. US official characterised strategy as "mowing the lawn" (Axios).

Why it matters: "Mowing the lawn" language is operationally significant: it signals a periodic, indefinite strike rhythm — not a decisive campaign endpoint. CENTCOM's explicit targeting of mine-laying capabilities attempts to degrade IRGC's primary Hormuz-disruption vector, but the IRGC has repeatedly reconstituted this capability (Larak mine-rocket prep confirmed). The retaliatory cycle is now self-sustaining at the structural level. No Threat-5 trigger met (confirmed US base casualties OR Gulf-state energy infrastructure damage), but the 72h window from 1 Sep is the highest-risk period for IRGC follow-on.

Implication: Threat-4 maintained; Threat-5 watch elevated. IRGC "many times greater" retaliatory commitment moves W1 watchlist item to highest priority. Periodic CENTCOM strike waves (~7–21 day cadence) now modelled as structural feature rather than escalation spikes. P(CENTCOM strike in any 7-day window) ~0.55.

Outlook

Severe → Confidence: Medium
  • Sc C (Re-escalation / multi-vector) now modal at 52%: IRGC "many times greater" retaliatory commitment; CENTCOM "mowing the lawn" (periodic indefinite strikes); Brent $90+ structural; corridor kinetically active; Houthi activation pressure.
  • Sc B (Frozen attrition) at 36%: CENTCOM periodic-strike + IRGC reconstitution equilibrium could persist indefinitely without triggering Threat-5 criteria; Jordan/Bahrain interception success creates partial deterrence buffer.
  • Signal against trend (anti-confirmation bias check): Jordan intercepted 3 of 4 IRGC missiles 1–2 Sep; Bahrain thwarted its wave; no confirmed US base casualties; Kharg not struck; Bessent cited 17Mb through Hormuz on 1 Sep as evidence of continued throughput; Brent $94.19 on 2 Sep (down slightly from intraday ~$95).

Why it matters: The system is in a self-sustaining attrition equilibrium. CENTCOM strikes degrade IRGC mine/radar capability; IRGC reconstitutes and retaliates; CENTCOM strikes again. Neither side has hit the other's red-line threshold (US casualties / Kharg strike). This equilibrium is stable in the short run but unstable over 30 days: each cycle increases the probability of an accidental escalation that crosses a threshold. The "mowing the lawn" framing suggests the US administration has accepted this equilibrium as manageable — which is itself a risk factor, as it reduces crisis urgency on the diplomatic side.

Implication: Sc A = 12%; Sc B = 36%; Sc C = 52% (sums to 100%). Supply-chain posture: Cape unconditional; no re-entry planning; forward inventory buffer 30–45 days recommended for Gulf-origin refined products; war-risk insurance costs locked at elevated level for remainder of Q3. Friday review: assess whether Jordan/Bahrain interception success warrants any Sc B upward revision.

Methodology

This brief uses a tiered signal-weighting framework. Only verified physical events from primary sources can move the threat assessment; trend movement requires confirmed change versus the prior 72h. The system continuously calibrates against outcomes — when predictions miss, the underlying heuristics are sharpened. The full operating ruleset is kept private to preserve analytical edge against adversarial reading.

Sources

Inputs span six tiers, weighted by track record. Specific outlets and platforms are not disclosed publicly.

  • Tier 1 — Primary & official. Maritime authorities, defense commands, foreign ministries, treasury sanctions desks. First-party communiqués that count as Hard signal.
  • Tier 2 — Specialized commercial intelligence. Vessel-tracking platforms (AIS-based), aircraft-tracking platforms (ADS-B), tanker-flow analytics, energy-cargo intelligence, piracy-reporting bureaus. Operational data with measurable track records.
  • Tier 3 — Analytical institutions. Geopolitical and security think tanks with rigorous publication standards and demonstrable calibration history on this region.
  • Tier 4 — Commercial analysts & columnists. Independent practitioners and commodity-desk analysts whose published positions can be back-tested over multi-year horizons.
  • Tier 5 — Wire services & reputable regional press. Western wire services, top-tier business and political dailies, regional papers across the Gulf, Israel, and Iran (read with editorial-line caveats), and analytical defense-and-policy publications.
  • Tier 6 — Excluded by default. Anonymous OSINT accounts, op-eds, social media, "sources say" without attribution, AI-generated summaries, forum content. Enter the brief only after independent corroboration by Tier 1–3.
