On 16 September Houthi forces claimed a large-scale ballistic-missile and drone attack on Saudi Aramco's Yanbu export terminal and Khamis Mushait airbase; NASA satellite imagery had already detected two ~40 MW thermal anomalies at the Yanbu complex on 15 September, and Saudi Civil Defence issued alerts covering Yanbu, Jeddah, Makkah, Taif, Abha and Jizan — but Aramco and the Saudi Energy Ministry have not yet confirmed damage, keeping the event at unconfirmed/Soft tier for operational purposes. The East-West pipeline remains offline on day 7: Bloomberg (16 Sep) reports Aramco is pursuing a partial bypass targeting roughly half capacity within days, with full restoration taking approximately six weeks, contradicting the prior brief's mis-scored "full restoration" assessment. Brent retreated from its $109.21 peak (15 Sep) to trade $104–106 on 17 Sep as markets weighed partial pipeline recovery against Yanbu terminal risk and a confirmed Hormuz transit count of just 8/day. A Hard Yanbu damage confirmation, formal Houthi Bab al-Mandeb closure declaration, or CENTCOM strike wave would individually be sufficient to trigger Threat-5; all three remain live within a 7-day window.

Maritime Security

Crisis ↑ Conf: High

  • Hormuz: 8 transits on 13 Sep vs 85/day pre-crisis baseline (IMF PortWatch via straits.live T3); 355 AIS-visible vessels holding position in Hormuz/Gulf watch box; Iranian hull blacklist at 77 vessels added in one week; no P&I club/flag state public response
  • MARAD-004 (Hard T1) active; Iranian "prohibited zone" coordinates still unpublished day 10+; Iran's blacklist is the operational control instrument, not a published exclusion zone

Why it matters: Hormuz is structurally closed to commercial shipping. The Iranian hull blacklist — not a formal closure declaration — is the operational mechanism, and it has grown faster than any single underwriter or flag state has responded publicly. Mine hazard cannot be cleared without a coordinated sanitisation protocol that does not yet exist.

Implication: No commercial vessel should transit without NCAGS escort and explicit Iran clearance. P&I clubs are implicitly pricing all Hormuz transits as war-risk regardless of flag. Treat strait as mine-hazard pending Hard sanitisation confirmation.

Implication: Do not action FM cascade on Houthi claim alone. Await Aramco/Saudi Energy Ministry Hard statement OR Kpler/Vortexa satellite volume confirmation of continued zero exports post-16 Sep. Confidence gap: Houthi claims on Aramco have ~60% Hard confirmation rate within 48h when satellite thermal + Civil Defence alerts both present (both met here).

Infrastructure & Energy

Crisis ↑ Conf: High
  • East-West pipeline: day 7 offline (struck 10 Sep); Aramco bypassing damaged section targeting ~3.5 mb/d partial capacity "within days"; full 7 mb/d restoration ~6 weeks (Bloomberg Hard T1, 16 Sep); PBS: officials briefed on 3–5 week repair timeline including "a major pumping facility"
  • Yanbu export terminal: zero Saudi crude departures since 11 Sep (Vortexa/Reuters); Houthi claimed mass strike 16 Sep; satellite fires 15 Sep; Aramco informed European customers some September cargoes cancelled; Rystad: 3 mb/d Yanbu loadings lost; 78–120 mb cumulative if 1-month outage
  • Brent: $109.21 peak 15 Sep (TradingEconomics T2); retreating to $104–106 on 17 Sep (Investing.com T2) as partial bypass news offsets Yanbu strike risk; Khurais 300k b/d still offline; Libya supply halt adding marginal pressure; US crude inventories fell 640k barrels week ending 11 Sep

Why it matters: The pipeline partial bypass represents the most significant near-term price relief available, but it delivers only ~3.5 mb/d vs the 7 mb/d capacity — and if the Yanbu terminal itself is Hard-damaged, even that partial flow has nowhere to load. The combination would constitute a triple-pathway blockade covering Hormuz, pipeline, and Red Sea export terminal simultaneously.

Implication: Brent $104–110 structural band while bypass proceeds and Yanbu status is unconfirmed. Yanbu Hard damage = $110+ floor, Goldman $120 tail active. Maintain hedge through at least EOD 19 Sep; do not close on Brent retreat alone given Yanbu uncertainty and pipeline re-attack risk from Iraq-based drones.

Alternative Routes

Crisis ↑ Conf: High
  • Houthi territorial control: entire Yemen Red Sea coast + Perim Island + Hanish Islands — direct-fire artillery covers both BAM channels; 99% Saudi-flagged vessels sheltering in Saudi territorial waters (Windward T3, 11 Sep); HRW documented three Houthi attacks on commercial vessels 20 Jul–24 Aug as potential war crimes
  • Yanbu terminal: zero exports since 11 Sep; Houthi claimed mass strike 16 Sep on Aramco Yanbu facility; Saudi Civil Defence alerts across entire Red Sea city corridor; partial pipeline bypass will arrive at a terminal under claimed attack
  • Cape of Good Hope: dominant reroute; tanker rates at elevated levels; +14–21 days to European/Asian buyers; port congestion building at Cape Town, Algoa Bay; Suez northbound near-zero Saudi crude; no MARAD-006 Bab al-Mandeb formal notice but operational exclusion de facto

Why it matters: The Red Sea route — which was handling 8× normal Saudi crude volumes March–July — has been progressively degraded through Houthi blockade declaration (20 Jul), pipeline attack (10 Sep), and now claimed terminal strike (16 Sep). The Cape is not a chokepoint, but it is a cost multiplier and its throughput capacity faces saturation risk at sustained diversion levels.

Implication: Cape unconditional for all Saudi-origin cargo. UAE Fujairah bypass (ADNOC) and Iraqi Basra remain partial alternatives but face their own threat elevations. Monitor Cape port congestion as saturation indicator — a 30-day sustained diversion at current levels approaches port congestion thresholds at major South African facilities.

Sources: Windward MIOC (T3) 11 Sep · Al Jazeera (T1) 11 Sep · Arab News (T2) 16 Sep · HRW (T2) via GlobalSecurity · MARAD-004 (Hard T1) · Wikipedia Bab al-Mandab crisis (T3) updated 17 Sep

Methodology

This brief uses a tiered signal-weighting framework. Only verified physical events from primary sources can move the threat assessment; trend movement requires confirmed change versus the prior 72h. The system continuously calibrates against outcomes — when predictions miss, the underlying heuristics are sharpened. The full operating ruleset is kept private to preserve analytical edge against adversarial reading.

Sources

Inputs span six tiers, weighted by track record. Specific outlets and platforms are not disclosed publicly.

  • Tier 1 — Primary & official. Maritime authorities, defense commands, foreign ministries, treasury sanctions desks. First-party communiqués that count as Hard signal.
  • Tier 2 — Specialized commercial intelligence. Vessel-tracking platforms (AIS-based), aircraft-tracking platforms (ADS-B), tanker-flow analytics, energy-cargo intelligence, piracy-reporting bureaus. Operational data with measurable track records.
  • Tier 3 — Analytical institutions. Geopolitical and security think tanks with rigorous publication standards and demonstrable calibration history on this region.
  • Tier 4 — Commercial analysts & columnists. Independent practitioners and commodity-desk analysts whose published positions can be back-tested over multi-year horizons.
  • Tier 5 — Wire services & reputable regional press. Western wire services, top-tier business and political dailies, regional papers across the Gulf, Israel, and Iran (read with editorial-line caveats), and analytical defense-and-policy publications.
  • Tier 6 — Excluded by default. Anonymous OSINT accounts, op-eds, social media, "sources say" without attribution, AI-generated summaries, forum content. Enter the brief only after independent corroboration by Tier 1–3.
